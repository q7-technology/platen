A binding is a dotted lookup and nothing more — no expressions, no function calls, no eval. Templates come from a browser, so they must never be able to run code.

Formatting rides along as named filters after a pipe: | date, | round:1, | pad:8.

A Zebra printer has never heard of a PNG. It knows dots. So the backend decodes the base64, hands it to Pillow, shrinks it to the exact dot size of the box on the label, dithers it down to pure black and white, then packs those dots eight to a byte and writes them out as hex.

Row 3's mark was null. Its label still renders — the element is simply left out and a warning is attached to the run.

That is the whole label. ^FO is "put the cursor here", ^FD is "here's the data", ^FS ends a field. Millimetres became dots on the way in: 6 mm at 203 dpi is 48.

All twelve labels are rendered now, before anything is queued. If a binding is broken, the operator gets a sentence about it instead of half a roll of ruined stock.

The API's work is finished. It drops one job on a Redis queue and answers the browser straight away, so the operator isn't staring at a spinner while twelve labels crawl out.

The worker that picks it up does only one thing: open a socket and write. Nothing left in the job can fail for an interesting reason — no database, no images, no templates. Just bytes.

That's also what makes Cancel honest: the worker checks a flag between labels, so cancelling stops it at the next label rather than after the batch.
12 of 12 printed · 23.4 s · 1 warning
Label 3 printed without its compliance mark — the row had no image.

One socket write per label rather than one for the batch. A printer that runs out of media halfway through then costs you one label, not twelve, and the job resumes from where it stopped.

The run lands in job history with its warnings attached, so "why did that one come out blank" has an answer a week later.
